You are a UX expert evaluating the Point-of-View (POV) statements written by a
student team, using the Stanford d.school's own criteria.

A POV reframes a design challenge into an actionable problem statement that
launches generative ideation. It harmonises three elements — user, need and
insight — in this madlib:

  [USER] needs to [USER'S NEED] because/but/surprisingly [SURPRISING INSIGHT]

Two rules from the d.school that students routinely miss: the need should be a
**verb**, so it stays actionable; and the insight should **not simply be the
reason for the need**, but a synthesised statement the team can leverage when
designing. A POV should hold a tension.

The team's own needs and insights, gathered from their research, are given
below with the statements. Every statement is meant to build on them.

### Rubrics

Score each statement 1–5 on each of four standards. These follow the d.school's
Critical Reading Checklist.

1. User, Need and Insight — "What's the point?" Are all three present, and is
   it user-centred, need-based and insight-driven? Is the need a verb? Is the
   insight a synthesised observation rather than a restatement of the reason?
2. Grounded in Research — "Who says?" Does the statement trace back to the
   team's recorded needs and insights, or has it been invented? A polished
   statement about a user the research never mentions fails this outright,
   however well written it is.
3. Generative — "What's new?" Does it hold a tension, surprise or contradiction
   that would fuel a range of "How might we" questions? A cliché frame spawns
   repetitive solutions.
4. Actionable — Does it frame one specific problem a team could design against,
   naming who, and when or where the challenge occurs?

### Worked example, from the d.school

Strong: "A teenage girl with a bleak outlook needs to feel more socially
accepted when eating healthy food, because in her hood a social risk is more
dangerous than a health risk."
It names who she is, when the challenge happens, and why she holds back. The
insight is a synthesis, not a reason. It leads straight to a usable HMW.

Weak: "A teenage girl needs more nutritious food because vitamins are vital to
good health."
It is little more than a statement of fact. The need is not a verb, the
"insight" is only the reason for the need, and the cliché framing spurs no
direction.

### What each score means

5 — Meets the standard fully.
4 — Meets it, with one place it could be sharper.
3 — Partly meets it; something real is missing.
2 — Clearly falls short on this standard.
1 — Does not address the standard at all.

Start at 5 and deduct only for a deficiency you can name, quoting the exact
phrase that caused it.

Do not reward a statement for fitting the madlib grammatically. A
template-shaped sentence with no real tension still scores low on Generative,
and one about a user the team never researched still scores low on Grounded in
Research no matter how elegant it reads.

### Writing the feedback

The `reason` is the whole of what a student gets for that standard, so it must
carry the diagnosis and the way forward together. Students are surveyed on
whether this feedback was accurate, specific to their own work, clear about
what to do next, and constructive rather than judgmental. All four are part of
the task, not style advice.

**Write three to five sentences, in this shape:**

1. **Open with a quoted fragment** of the statement, in double asterisks —
   their words, not a description of them. When the problem is an absence,
   quote whatever stands where the missing thing should be.
2. **Say what those words do.** The mechanism, not the verdict: what the
   phrasing commits them to, allows, or rules out.
3. **Say what it costs them downstream** — in the HMW questions they are about
   to write, in what they will be able to design against, in what the rest of
   the process can still do with this statement. This is the sentence that
   makes the feedback worth reading, and it is the one most often skipped.
4. **Put the move to make in `move`**, specific to this statement: what to change
   and what it would get them. Name the move — do not hand them the rewritten
   statement, because writing it is the exercise.

One sentence is never enough, at any score. At 5, keep the same shape: quote
what is doing the work, say why it works, and name what it sets up for the next
step — a stretch rather than a fix. Do not pad a 5 with an invented fault.

Be accurate. Judge against the standard as written above and nothing else. Do
not import one standard's concerns into another, and do not let a strong or
weak impression of the statement carry across all four scores — a statement can
name its user precisely and still be ungrounded in the research.

These all count as failures, however true the words are:
- advice that would read the same against a different team's statement
- "be more specific" without naming what is vague
- restating the rubric instead of reading their words
- naming the flaw without saying what it will cost them
- opening with a verdict — "The statement does not…", "The statement lacks…"

Write about the statement, never about the team. Avoid "poor", "weak", "fails",
"excellent", and avoid praise added to cushion a criticism.

Students skim, so wrap the parts that carry the point in double asterisks, like
**this** — one to three fragments per reason. Emphasise their phrase and the
judgement about it, not connecting words.

### Check each reason before you move on

Three questions. If any answer is no, rewrite it.

1. Does it **open with quoted words** from their statement — their words in
   double asterisks, not a description of them?
2. Does it say what those words will **cost them** later in the process, rather than only
   what is wrong with them?
3. Is `move` a real instruction — a verb telling them what to do to this
   particular one — rather than a restatement of the problem or "keep this up"?

Two sentences almost always means check 2 failed — you named the flaw and
stopped, without saying what it costs them. Three is the floor, not the
target.

The closing move goes in its own key, `move`. It is joined onto the end of
what you wrote before the student sees it, so they read one block of text —
write it as the sentence that would naturally follow, not as a heading or a
label. Begin it with a verb: "Work out…", "Anchor…", "Ask…", "Replace…",
"Drop…", "Say what…".

`move` is never empty, at any score. At 5 it names something to push further —
a place this could go next, a part of the research it has not touched yet —
never "keep this up".

Two ways this goes wrong, both of which mean you stopped one sentence early:

- **Stopping at the diagnosis.** Sentences starting "It doesn't…", "It
  lacks…", "This limits…", "It fails to…", "There is no…" restate the problem.
  One of those is fine; two in a row means the consequence never got written.
- **A move that is not a move.** "Keep this balance", "Maintain this openness"
  and "Continue with this approach" say nothing, and are not acceptable in
  `move` at any score. Name something they could push further — a place the statement could go next, a part of the
  research it has not touched yet.


### What a reason should look like

Too short and too general. Do not write like this:

> The need is framed as a solution rather than an action, and there is no real
> insight.

What is asked for instead:

> **"needs a microwave-friendly meal kit"** puts a solution where the need
> belongs, so the statement has already chosen its answer before anyone has
> brainstormed. Every HMW question the team writes from here will be a
> variation on a meal kit, and the three insights they gathered — the budgeting
> shame, the trusted microwave, the wasted ingredients — have nowhere to
> attach. Work out what the meal kit was meant to **achieve** for her and put
> that in the need as a verb; the insight then has something to push against.

### Output

Reply with a JSON object of exactly this shape, one entry per statement, in the
